# STAT 19000: Data Science Through Experimentation 🔬

**Learning to know when a result is actually real, by testing it yourself, live, in class.**

## Overview

**Proposed under STAT 19000, Topics in Statistics for Undergraduates**, an existing
freshman-level special-topics course number already used for a rotating set of topics each
semester (e.g., The Data Mine I, First Year Statistics Seminar). This proposal is for a
specific topic offering under that number, not a request for a new permanent course.

This 15-week, 30-class course gives second-semester freshman Data Science majors an early,
hands-on introduction to experimentation and causal reasoning, the discipline underneath every
trustworthy data science result. You'll learn to formulate real questions from messy data,
tell the difference between a pattern and an illusion, run and interpret an experiment, and
communicate what you found to someone who isn't a statistician.

This is not a lecture course. Every class centers on something you do: a live class experiment,
a game, a head-to-head challenge, a real dataset put in front of you cold, because the fastest
way to learn when to trust a result is to get fooled by a fake one first, in a room full of
your classmates, and figure out why.

The semester builds toward one capstone: a real experiment or causal analysis you design, run,
and defend yourself.

## 📚 Course Structure

**Duration**: 15 weeks | **Classes**: 30 total (twice per week, Tuesday/Thursday)
**Format**: Interactive, activity-driven, minimal lecture
**Philosophy**: You don't understand a statistical trap until you've fallen into one
**Prerequisites**: Intro programming (Python) only, no calculus or prior stats required

### What You'll Build

- A running "fooled you" journal of statistical traps you personally fell for and caught
- A capstone experiment or causal analysis on a real dataset, designed and run by you
- A critique of an AI-generated data analysis, live-debated in class
- A final presentation defending your capstone's conclusion to a non-technical audience

## 🎯 Class-by-Class Journey

### Week 1 (Jan 11 to Jan 17): Welcome, and Getting Fooled

**Class 1: Course Introduction & Orientation** (Tue, Jan 12): Who this course is for, why it
exists, and what you'll walk away with. We'll cover expectations, the AI policy, how the
capstone works, and do a quick icebreaker. No coding today, just get oriented.

**Class 2: The Coin Flip That Wasn't Fair** (Thu, Jan 14): Class-wide coin flip tournament.
Some coins are rigged, some aren't. Using only the results, figure out which is which, no
formulas allowed yet. This is the moment the course's whole point clicks.

### Week 2 (Jan 18 to Jan 24): Asking a Good Question
*No class Monday, January 18 (MLK Jr. Day); this does not affect our Tue/Thu schedule.*

**Class 3: What Makes a Question Answerable** (Tue, Jan 19): Turning a vague curiosity into
something you can actually investigate with data.

**Class 4: Speed-Dating Question Workshop** (Thu, Jan 21): Pitch your question to 3 classmates,
2 minutes each, refine it based on their pushback.

### Week 3 (Jan 25 to Jan 31): Exploring Data Honestly

**Class 5: Intro to EDA in Python** (Tue, Jan 26): Light exploratory data analysis with pandas
and simple plots.

**Class 6: Cold Dataset Challenge** (Thu, Jan 28): Given a dataset you've never seen, teams
race to find one real insight and one fake-looking-but-real oddity in 30 minutes.

### Week 4 (Feb 1 to Feb 7): Correlation Is Not Causation

**Class 7: Confounding, Explained Through Absurd Examples** (Tue, Feb 2): Classic spurious
correlations and why they happen.

**Class 8: Spot the Confounder** (Thu, Feb 4): Kahoot-style game using real news headlines.

### Week 5 (Feb 8 to Feb 14): Comparing Groups the Right Way

**Class 9: Why Before/After Comparisons Mislead** (Tue, Feb 9): Selection effects, regression
to the mean.

**Class 10: The Before/After Debate** (Thu, Feb 11): Two teams argue for or against a real
"before/after" claim; class votes, then the reveal.

### Week 6 (Feb 15 to Feb 21): Introduction to Hypothesis Testing

**Class 11: What a Hypothesis Test Actually Answers** (Tue, Feb 16): Taught through simulation,
not formulas.

**Class 12: Run Your Own Live Experiment** (Thu, Feb 18): The class runs a real experiment on
itself (e.g., does background music change puzzle-solving speed?) and analyzes its own data.

### Week 7 (Feb 22 to Feb 28): Confidence and Uncertainty

**Class 13: What a Confidence Interval Really Means** (Tue, Feb 23)

**Class 14: Guess the Range** (Thu, Feb 25): Teams estimate confidence intervals for hidden
quantities, scored on both accuracy and honesty about uncertainty.

### Week 8 (Mar 1 to Mar 7): Designing a Simple Experiment

**Class 15: Anatomy of a Clean Experiment** (Tue, Mar 2): Control groups, randomization, what
can still go wrong.

**Class 16: Design-a-Flaw Game** (Thu, Mar 4): Teams are given a "finished" experiment design
and compete to find its most damaging flaw.

### Week 9 (Mar 8 to Mar 14): Capstone Kickoff

**Class 17: Scoping a Project You Can Actually Finish** (Tue, Mar 9): How to pick a question
and dataset sized for five weeks of work.

**Class 18: Rapid-Fire Proposal Pitches** (Thu, Mar 11): 2-minute pitches with instant class
feedback.
**Milestone:** Capstone proposal due.

### Spring Break (Mar 15 to Mar 20)
No class. Enjoy the break.

### Week 10 (Mar 22 to Mar 28): Build Sprint I

**Class 19: Open Lab, Part 1** (Tue, Mar 23): In-class work time with instructor support.

**Class 20: Open Lab, Part 2 / Troubleshooting Clinic** (Thu, Mar 25): Continued build time,
focused on common real-data problems (missing values, outliers, small samples).

### Week 11 (Mar 29 to Apr 4): When AI Gets It Wrong

**Class 21: How AI Reasons About Statistics (and Where It Fails)** (Tue, Mar 30)

**Class 22: AI Jury Trials** (Thu, Apr 1): Juries "convict or acquit" a flawed AI-generated
analysis, live, with reasoning.

### Week 12 (Apr 5 to Apr 11): Build Sprint II and Peer Review

**Class 23: Open Lab, Part 3** (Tue, Apr 6)

**Class 24: Peer Review Speed Rounds** (Thu, Apr 8): Every capstone reviewed by two classmates
in a single period.

### Week 13 (Apr 12 to Apr 18): Communicating Results

**Class 25: Explaining Results Without Jargon** (Tue, Apr 13)

**Class 26: Explain It to a Freshman, Lightning Round** (Thu, Apr 15): 90 seconds per person,
timed, no slides.
**Milestone:** Final capstone analysis due.

### Week 14 (Apr 19 to Apr 25): Capstone Showcase Prep

**Class 27: Presentation Skills Workshop** (Tue, Apr 20): Structuring a clear, honest
presentation.

**Class 28: Dry-Run Gauntlet** (Thu, Apr 22): Each presenter gets grilled by a rotating
"hot seat" panel of classmates.

### Week 15 (Apr 26 to May 1): Capstone Showcase

**Class 29: Capstone Showcase, Part 1** (Tue, Apr 27)

**Class 30: Capstone Showcase, Part 2, and Course Wrap-Up** (Thu, Apr 29): Final presentations
conclude; reflect on the semester's throughline, from getting fooled by a rigged coin in
Class 2 to defending your own real result here.

## 🎯 Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, you'll be able to:

✅ Formulate a clear, answerable question from a messy real-world dataset

✅ Spot common traps that make comparisons misleading, because you've personally fallen for
   several of them, live, in class
   
✅ Run and correctly interpret a basic hypothesis test or causal comparison

✅ Critically evaluate an AI-generated data analysis

✅ Design, execute, and defend a real capstone experiment or causal analysis

✅ Communicate a data-driven conclusion, and its limitations, to a non-technical audience

---

## 🌟 What Makes This Course Special

**Get Fooled First.** Every trap you'll learn to avoid, you'll fall into at least once, live,
in a low-stakes class setting, that's what makes it stick.

**Almost No Lecture.** Class time is built around games, live experiments, debates, and
workshops. If you can sit back and just listen, we've done it wrong.

**A Real Capstone.** Not a worksheet, a genuine experiment or causal analysis you design,
build, defend, and can put on a resume or grad school application.

**AI Literacy as a Skill, Not a Rule.** AI is treated as a tool to learn to use well, including
a dedicated activity where the class collectively tears apart a flawed AI analysis.

This course prepares students for:
- Upper-level statistics and data science coursework, with real intuition already built
- Internship and research opportunities that expect basic experimental literacy
- A skeptical, evidence-first mindset that transfers to any data-driven field
